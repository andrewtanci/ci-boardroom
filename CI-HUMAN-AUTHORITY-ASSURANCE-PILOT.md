# CI — Human Authority Assurance Pilot Protocol

**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 16 Sep 2026

## Purpose

Keep CI's durable assurance thesis separate from the Boardroom answer-quality benchmark.

The Boardroom benchmark tests whether CI produces better decision support.

This pilot protocol tests a different question:

> Can CI produce credible, auditable evidence that a named human genuinely exercised authority over consequential AI-supported work?

This is the proof route for the broader CI / AI TAP assurance direction. It cannot be established by prompt benchmarks alone.

---

## 1. Core Claim Under Test

Do not claim that a human remained in control merely because a workflow contains an approval button.

The stronger claim requires evidence that the human:

- knew they were accountable for the decision;
- received enough information to exercise judgment;
- could realistically identify material AI errors or uncertainty;
- had meaningful ability to challenge, change, reject, pause or escalate;
- actually used that authority where warranted;
- did not merely rubber-stamp the system;
- left a traceable record of the final human action and basis.

---

## 2. Pilot Unit

Each pilot should follow one real, bounded workflow where AI materially assists a consequential human task.

Examples may include:

- assessment/marking support;
- hiring support;
- client recommendation preparation;
- compliance/governance review support;
- management decision preparation;
- approval or exception workflows.

The pilot should not begin with a workflow so broad that human authority cannot be observed clearly.

---

## 3. Evidence to Capture

For each consequential AI-supported item, record where lawful and appropriate:

- workflow/case ID;
- date/time;
- AI role and delegated boundary;
- named human decision-owner;
- evidence/source material available to the AI;
- AI output/recommendation;
- uncertainty, source gaps and escalation flags shown to the human;
- evidence/source material available to the human;
- human action: accept / modify / reject / pause / escalate;
- human changes and rationale where practical;
- material AI error found by the human;
- material AI error missed by the human and found later;
- whether the human had enough domain knowledge/time/context to catch the error;
- final decision/action;
- downstream outcome where observable;
- audit trail completeness.

Protect personal, confidential and regulated information. Use anonymisation or controlled storage where required.

---

## 4. Human-Authority Tests

A workflow does not pass merely because a human clicked approve.

Test:

### A. Decision ownership
Can a named human state what decision they own and what the AI is allowed to do?

### B. Comprehension
Did the human receive the material evidence, uncertainty and competing considerations needed to judge the AI output?

### C. Challengeability
Could the human question the AI, inspect sources, request alternatives or identify missing evidence?

### D. Overrideability
Could the human change, reject, pause or escalate without artificial friction or punishment?

### E. Error detectability
Were material errors presented in a form a competent human could realistically detect?

### F. Actual intervention
Across a meaningful sample, did humans ever modify, reject or escalate AI output? A 100% approval rate is not proof of good oversight and may be a warning sign.

### G. Traceability
Can an auditor/reviewer reconstruct what the AI proposed, what the human saw, what the human decided and why?

---

## 5. Pilot Metrics

Track separately rather than hiding everything in one score:

- % of consequential cases with named human owner;
- % with material sources/evidence available to the human;
- % with uncertainty/source gaps exposed;
- human modification rate;
- human rejection/escalation rate;
- AI material-error rate;
- human detection rate for seeded or later-confirmed material errors;
- human miss rate;
- time required for meaningful review;
- rubber-stamp indicators;
- override friction;
- traceability completeness;
- repeat-error rate after a failure is converted into a control/rule.

The goal is not to maximise intervention. The goal is to demonstrate that intervention is real, proportionate and possible when needed.

---

## 6. Error-Detection Challenge

Where ethically and operationally safe, include controlled test items containing known AI errors or missing evidence so the pilot can measure whether human oversight actually detects them.

Do not use harmful hidden errors in live high-consequence decisions. Use sandbox, retrospective or clearly controlled test conditions.

This is stronger evidence than asking staff whether they 'feel in control.'

---

## 7. Pilot Comparison

Where feasible compare:

1. current workflow without CI controls;
2. AI-assisted workflow with ordinary human approval;
3. AI-assisted workflow with CI authority/evidence controls.

Compare not only accuracy but:

- error detection;
- time burden;
- quality of evidence available to the human;
- intervention quality;
- auditability;
- decision-owner clarity;
- user friction.

CI fails commercially if assurance becomes control-by-annoyance.

---

## 8. Independent Review

For serious claims, have an independent governance/audit/domain practitioner inspect a sample of pilot records and answer:

- Was human authority genuine or ceremonial?
- Was the evidence sufficient for the human to judge?
- Were important AI errors reasonably detectable?
- Is the audit trail enough to reconstruct the decision?
- What would make the assurance claim misleading?

CI authors should not be the sole reviewers of their own authority evidence.

---

## 9. Claim Discipline

Before sufficient pilots, say:

> CI is designed to make human authority visible, challengeable and auditable in AI-supported work. We are validating whether those controls produce stronger evidence of genuine human oversight in real workflows.

Do not say:

> CI proves every AI decision had meaningful human oversight.

No pilot proves every future workflow.

---

## 10. Strategic Separation

### Boardroom benchmark
Proves or disproves **Claim A:** better decision support.

### Assurance pilots
Prove or disprove **Claim B:** auditable human authority.

### Durable moat hypothesis
Public decision rules can be copied. A harder-to-copy advantage may come from:

- integrated workflow controls;
- accumulated authority/failure evidence;
- assurance methodology;
- auditability;
- practitioner network;
- deployment know-how;
- longitudinal outcome/error data;
- trusted positioning around accountable human authority.

This remains a hypothesis until validated by pilots and market adoption.

---

## 11. First Pilot Rule

The first two external pilots matter disproportionately because they are the first evidence that the failure-learning pipeline works on people other than the founder.

For each pilot, capture at least one real or controlled failure mode supplied by the client/practitioner, convert it into a generic CI control/test, and verify whether the updated workflow prevents recurrence.

That is the transition from one-person QA to an accumulating external assurance system.
